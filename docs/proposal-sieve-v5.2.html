A Phase Diagram for KV Cache Compression

Uniform quantization, per-token rate allocation, and token eviction are each optimal in a different regime. The boundary variable is derivable from the same cost comparison that generates the theory, it contains no context length, and context length is what moves models across it.

Thesis. Every KV compression method minimizes a reconstruction distortion. Attention does not consume keys; it consumes an output \(o=\sum_i a_iv_i\). Posed against output distortion, KV compression becomes a rate-allocation problem whose solution is reverse water-filling, with eviction recovered as the zero-rate case at derived cost \(c_0=1\). The three families are therefore one program — and the program tells you which of them to use.

What changed in v9. Three things, all of which cost us numbers and bought defensibility. (i) The comparison is now symmetric: v8 demoted the eviction corner to a deployable score but left our interior reading the current step. With both sides lagged, the edge survives at about half its size and 5 of 16 cells are STOP, where v8 reported none (§1). (ii) The boundary is pinned per architecture, not universally (§2) — dead-tier fraction still orders the band almost perfectly, but its critical value is architecture-specific, so C1 is restated as an ordering claim plus a fitted threshold. (iii) The design is no longer a sketch: allocation is per KV head (realizable, measured cost 1.14–1.32×), the score is a cascade off the 4-bit base tier, and the router and the allocator run on different clocks (§5, §6). What is still open is the one thing a reviewer cares most about: end-task accuracy (§8), now in progress.

1. The symmetric grid — what the interior is worth when both sides are deployable

Per-head measurement at 4k–262k context on H100s: PG-19 haystacks, real TurboQuantmse quantized keys, exact recomputation of the attention output, gains against the better of the two corners at matched total bits. Every configuration passes an input-validity gate requiring real text and demonstrated needle retrieval.

The correction that defines this version — our own interior was still an oracle v8 rebuilt the eviction corner from real evictors (H2O / SnapKV / StreamingLLM on lagged attention) and reported that every STOP verdict disappeared. But the interior still water-filled on the current step's sensitivity \(a_i\lVert v_i-o\rVert\) — the same privilege we had just published as unacceptable in a baseline. The symmetric cell fixes that: both sides score on lagged attention, with positions the score has never seen held at the top tier rather than evicted. Demoting the corner alone (v8's "E2" cell) adds 6.4–28.7 band points over the all-oracle cell; demoting the interior as well gives back 42% of that (median cell). The interior still wins on 16/16 cells, by +1.2 to +12.5 points over the all-oracle comparison — but the honest grid has STOP cells in it again.

1.1 Where the interior's cost lands — the finding that makes the design

The interior does not lose uniformly when it is denied the current step. It loses exactly on the heads it was winning:

interior lag cost \(=\) err(lagged) / err(current-step)value
median head1.00–1.28×
out-of-band heads (gain < 2×)1.00–1.04×
in-band heads (gain ≥ 2×)1.13–1.64×
\(\rho\)(lag cost, gain), 16/16 cells+0.50 … +0.97

A head is in band precisely because its sensitivity is concentrated somewhere a flat allocation cannot exploit — and that concentration is what moves between steps. The interior's advantage and its dependence on current-query information are the same property. This single fact drives the whole design in §5–§6: it says the allocation must be re-budgeted (and §6 prices the alternative), and it says a cheap current-query score is worth building (§5.2). The lagged allocator still evicts 39–54% of tokens on its own, against 40–54% for the oracle one — tier 0 is inside the allocator either way.

The price of deployable scoring falls with context. accum-corner error / oracle-corner error is 1.29–1.47× at 8k, 1.12–1.42× at 32k, 1.03–1.14× at 128k, in 6/6 models: at long context a lagged score is nearly as good as current-step truth for deciding what to keep. C5's "scoring is solved" is strongest exactly where the band is lowest.

2. Two boundaries — and the one that is architecture-specific

Both edges fall out of comparing \(\tau^2c_b\) with the derived eviction cost \(c_0=1\): the ladder collapses at the diffuse end (\(\tau/\ln2\) too narrow for the allocation to differ from uniform), and tiers go extinct at the sharp end (\(\tau^2c_b>1\) kills rungs from the bottom until two tiers remain, and two tiers is eviction). The 1-bit tier is dead in 95–100% of heads everywhere and should never be offered.

Where the boundary sits — 25 cells, six models, isotonic (monotone) crossings. A parametric fit is the wrong tool here and we say so: the logistic put GO at 34.6% when no cell existed between 25.8% and 27.0%, so the reader gets a crossing that cannot leave its own bracket, plus two intervals — one for measurement, one for architecture.

C1, restated — this is a correction, not a caveat Nine cells were run specifically to close these brackets. They did not close them; they turned the GO bracket from a gap (missing data) into an overlap (contradictory data). At dead-2 ≈ 54, llama3.1-8B @128k reads 20.2% in band while qwen3-8B @16k reads 12.2% — an 8-point disagreement across 0.6 points of the phase variable. Per-model crossings span 5.4 points (llama3.1-8B 26.7, llama3.3-70B 31.9, qwen1.5-MoE 32.1) and the architecture interval is ~1.5× the measurement interval at both lines. More cells cannot fix this: it is architecture scatter, not noise. So C1 becomes an ordering claim — dead-2 ranks the band almost perfectly, \(\rho=-0.978\) pooled and −1.00 within every model — plus a per-architecture threshold that the calibration pass must fit, which it already has the data to do. A universal "45%" number would have been wrong in both directions.

R4 · is the long-context collapse real, or an artifact of running models at their trained limit? · ANSWERED Two readings fit the old data equally well: the decay is a property of attention (ABSOLUTE-L), or part of it is an artifact of measuring at 100% of the RoPE window (ROPE-FRAC). The test was pre-registered before the cells ran: read \(\tau\), not dead-2 rates, against a quadratic-in-\(\log_2 L\) null fitted to each model's pre-cap points, with thresholds fixed in advance (excess > +0.25 = cap effect, |excess| < 0.15 = smooth). Cells at 196k and 262k were run on the one model with RoPE headroom, and every one passed its needle gate.

The answer is mostly ABSOLUTE-L, and it reverses this project's earlier reading. \(\tau\) is convex in \(\log L\), so the linear extrapolations that produced the original "cap effect" under-predicted and manufactured one; dead-2 rates are a threshold artifact. What survives is a real but modest +0.21 \(\tau\) excess at exactly rope_frac = 1.00 (qwen3-30B +0.209, qwen3-8B +0.216), none at 0.75 (+0.028) and none in llama3.1-8B (−0.135). An out-of-sample check: the null fitted without the 64k point predicted \(\tau=2.611\) there and the measured value was 2.563.

Consequence for the paper: the "37-point collapse in the last octave" headline of v8 is withdrawn as a mechanism claim. The decay is real, steep and smooth in \(\log L\); models measured at their exact trained limit carry a small additional penalty on top.

4. The allocation theorem

With \(\hat s_i=s_i+\delta_i\), \(\operatorname{Var}(\delta_i)=\tau^2c_b\), and \(\partial o/\partial s_i=a_i(v_i-o)\), first-order output error is \(\sum_i a_i^2w_i^2\tau^2c_{b_i}\). Minimizing under \(\sum_ib_i\le B\) gives, above the water level,

$$b_i^\star=\log_2 a_i+c \qquad\text{(the } \|v_i-o\| \text{ factor is measured to be negligible, ≤ 0.035 bits)}$$

and \(b_i^\star=0\) otherwise. Eviction's cost constant is derived, not chosen: dropping a set \(E\) and renormalizing costs exactly \(\sum_{i\in E}a_i^2\|o_R-v_i\|^2\), so \(c_0=1\) in absolute units and eviction sits on the same axis as every quantized tier. The comparison \(\tau^2c_b\) vs \(1\) generates the tier-extinction boundary of §2.

The theory's one genuine forward prediction remains the linearized cost model against exactly-recomputed gain: 0.80–1.42× on per-configuration medians over the 27-cell symmetric grid (0.79–1.34 over v8's 24 cells). The former headline "\(\tau/\ln2\) confirmed to 1.5%" is an algebraic identity (\(\log_2a_i = s_i/\ln2-\log_2Z\)), verified at \(8\times10^{-16}\) and demoted to an appendix consistency check.

5. The design, now measured rather than sketched

v8 left three architecture questions open, each of which could have invalidated the method as a system rather than as an analysis. All three are now measured.

5.1 Per-head rates are realizable under GQA — at a discount that scales with group size

Five of six models are grouped-query: heads sharing a KV head cannot be given independent rates, so "per-head allocation" is partly fictional unless the shared constraint is cheap. The kill line written before the run was 3×. Measured on in-band heads, with the lag cost divided out:

Dose–response 1.000 → 1.136 → 1.316 at n_rep 1 → 4 → 8 makes this a mechanism rather than a number: heads that share a KV head are alike but not identical, and the more of them share, the more the compromise costs. The n_rep = 1 control is exact to the decimal, which is what licenses reading the other rows as the grouping's own cost. Two consequences the reviewer should not miss: across all heads the cost is 1.003–1.056× (it concentrates on the heads that matter, exactly like the lag cost of §1.1); and the shared constraint hurts eviction MORE than it hurts the interior — the group band sits 4.3–6.5 points above the per-head band, because one keep-set shared by n_rep heads is a harsher compromise than one set of bit-widths. Under the realizable constraint the interior's edge widens.

5.2 The score: a cascade off the base tier closes ~89% of the lag penalty

§1.1 showed the interior's cost is precisely its dependence on the current query. The cascade pays that back without reading full-precision keys: score the current query against the 4-bit base tier that is already stored, then allocate.

5.3 Timing: the router and the allocator run on different clocks

C4 claims one offline calibration pass. Six cells, two architectures, 8k–128k, sampled and greedy, generating 4,096 tokens with a sparse measurement schedule:

  • The route survives the whole generation. p90 regret from keeping the calibration-time route is 1.00 in all six cells. Route flips run 9–25% against a same-run noise floor of 6–23%, and rank correlation of gain against the calibration step holds at 0.63–0.83. C4's offline pass is verified.
  • The allocation does not. Keeping the prefill-time allocation costs 2.5–4.3× the error of a one-step-old one by the end of each context's valid horizon.

This is a two-timescale system, and that is a design result, not a caveat: route once, offline, per head; re-budget the bits during decode. It also explains why the cascade of §5.2 matters — re-budgeting is what needs a current-query score that does not read full keys.

One honest negative from the same campaign: whether \(\tau\) drifts within a generation because of content or because the cache grows cannot be told apart at 3–6 prompts (per-prompt sd ≈ 0.22). An earlier claim that it matched the cache-growth prediction is withdrawn as underpowered; it needs ~20 prompts per cell.

7. End-task accuracy — the open item, and its first result

Every number above is an output-error ratio. The fair question is whether that matters, and it is the one thing this paper still owes. The experiment (R8) is built and running: RULER-style retrieval and multi-hop tasks on the same PG-19 haystacks, the model generating from a genuinely compressed cache (simulated quantization at the allocated widths, evicted tokens masked), arms at matched bits — full precision, uniform, SnapKV, H2O, the water-fill interior, and the router against its own oracle.

P0 — the budget pilot, complete (llama3.1-8B @32k, 20 prompts × 4 tasks): a negative result worth reporting Full precision answers 0.99–1.00 on every task, so the tasks are valid. Uniform quantization fails at 1 bit and is perfect from 2 bits — a cliff, exactly where the dead-2 fraction predicts it for this model. But SnapKV scores 1.00 at every budget down to 1 bit, on every task. The reason is structural and it is a finding about the literature's evaluation protocol, not about our allocator: when the question sits inside the observation window that the evictor scores with, eviction is an oracle on retrieval tasks. The window vote points straight at the needle, and nothing else can beat perfect.

The fix, now built: compress the context before the question exists — the regime of prefix caching and multi-turn serving, and the one where a unified quantize-or-evict allocator should matter, because mixed precision degrades a needle it did not foresee instead of deleting it. In this mode a blind SnapKV keeps a recency-shifted set (mean kept position 0.61 → 0.75 of the context) and loses needles that are not near the end. A 0.5-bit budget is added below uniform's cliff, where eviction and the interior are the only arms that exist. P0b is the next job; the interior and router arms follow.

8. Contributions

  1. The objective is wrong. KV compression should minimize attention-output distortion, not key reconstruction distortion.
  2. Eviction is quantization at zero rate; \(c_0=1\) is derived, not posited, putting two literatures on one axis.
  3. A phase diagram whose boundary variable is derived rather than fitted, and which survives two changes of baseline. The dead-tier fraction orders the productive band at \(\rho=-0.978\) over 25 configurations, six architectures and 4k–262k of context, −1.00 within every model, −0.915 partialled on model identity. It contains no \(L\). It was established against an oracle corner and survived that corner being replaced (+6 to +29 points) and then our own interior being demoted as well (−42% of that gain). Restated in v9: it is an ordering claim plus an architecture-specific threshold — the crossing spans 5.4 points across models, which nine further cells sharpened rather than closed.
  4. Context length is a phase variable, acting through \(\tau\), and \(\tau\) is convex in \(\log L\) (v9 correction; the earlier "accelerating collapse" was linear extrapolation plus a threshold artifact). One model traverses GO → NARROW on context alone. Models measured at 100% of their RoPE window carry an additional +0.21 \(\tau\) excess, measured against a pre-registered curved null on the one model with headroom.
  5. A cost model that forward-predicts measured output-error gain to 0.80–1.42× over 27 configurations, independent of the corner definition. The \(\tau/\ln2\) "prediction" it replaced is an algebraic identity (§4).
  6. The price of deployable information, measured on both sides. For the corner: current-step truth is worth 1.03–1.47× over a lagged score, falling with context. For the interior: 1.13–1.64× on in-band heads and 1.00–1.04× elsewhere, with \(\rho(\text{lag cost},\text{gain})=+0.50\ldots+0.97\). The interior's advantage and its need for current-query information are the same property — which is what makes the two-timescale design necessary rather than convenient.
  7. Per-head rates are realizable under GQA, and the constraint favours us. 1.000 / 1.136 / 1.316× at n_rep 1 / 4 / 8 (kill line 3×), monotone in group size, with an exact n_rep = 1 control; and the same constraint costs eviction 4.3–6.5 band points more than it costs the interior.
  8. A two-timescale result for per-head routing. The route survives 4,096 generated tokens (p90 regret 1.00, six cells); the allocation does not (2.5–4.3× if frozen). A cascade score off the 4-bit base tier closes ~89% of the resulting penalty without reading full keys.
  9. Tail dependence is architectural and spans 36×. \(K^\*\) is 100% of the fractional budget everywhere (the absolute-support budget we proposed is refuted); \(K^\*/n_{95}\) spans 6.5–233 and is not ordered by support.
  10. A methodological result about measuring attention, including the error budget of our own verdict statistic: the corner set moves the band 5–9 points and changes verdicts, while prompts move it 0.4–3.0. And a protocol finding for end-task evaluation (§7): a question-aware evictor is an oracle on retrieval benchmarks, which is how it scores 1.00 at every budget we tested.

11. The three ways this still dies

  1. End-task does not move (§7). If accuracy at matched bits is flat across arms — or if only a protocol nobody deploys can separate them — the diagram stays true and the method stays uninteresting. P0 already showed the default retrieval protocol cannot separate anything, because a question-aware evictor is perfect on it. The question-agnostic regime is the honest test; it is also the one where we expect to win, so it must be pre-registered and reported whichever way it lands.
  2. Long context is NARROW-to-STOP, and the corner set can push it over. On the symmetric grid 5 of 16 cells are STOP, and under the five-corner minimum llama3.1-8B @128k (20.5 → 14.5) straddles the STOP line. Allocation helps most below ~64k, where the KV cache hurts least. The science survives — the diagram predicts its own boundary — but the deployment reach narrows.
  3. Nesting is not free at finite \(d\). Equitz–Cover is asymptotic and assumes optimal vector quantization; we use per-coordinate Lloyd-Max at \(d{=}128\) with three refinement boundaries. A 20% rate penalty removes the storage architecture's foundation, and it is still unmeasured.
